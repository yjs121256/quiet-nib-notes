How to Store Fountain Pen Ink (and Inked Pens): Light, Temperature, Mold, and Sample Vials

Keep bottled ink cool, dark, upright, and clean. How to spot mold or 'slime,' how to decant samples safely, and what to do with pens you won't use for a while.

Fountain pen ink is less fragile than people fear and more fragile than people assume. A well-kept bottle can last for years, and a bottle on a sunny windowsill can spoil. Google’s suggestions for “fountain pen ink” include “ink storage” (Google autocomplete, checked 2026-11-22), and the answer is mostly common sense. Mold is the one hazard worth taking seriously.

Does fountain pen ink expire?

Not on a fixed date. Goldspot’s guide notes that manufacturing precautions against bacteria give ink “a very long shelf life,” and that ink becomes unusable through contamination or evaporation rather than a set expiry. How long that takes depends on how it’s stored (Goldspot). Pen Weekly explains that most fountain pen ink contains a preservative that “holds mould and bacteria at bay,” and that “a good bottle stays usable for years” when stored well (Pen Weekly).

Where to keep bottles

The same few principles appear across retailer guides:

  • Dark. Goldspot recommends keeping ink out of direct sunlight, ideally in its original box (Goldspot). Endless Pens notes that exposure to sunlight or UV light may desaturate or change ink colors over time (Endless Pens).
  • Cool and stable. Pen Weekly suggests a cool, stable spot, “roughly mid-teens to low twenties Celsius.” That rules out window ledges, radiators, lofts, garages, and car gloveboxes. It also warns that kitchens and bathrooms swing in temperature and humidity more than people expect (Pen Weekly). Goldspot similarly advises against attics and garages (Goldspot).
  • Upright and tightly capped. Endless Pens stresses sealing the cap tightly and keeping bottles upright to prevent evaporation, contamination, and leaks (Endless Pens). Pen Weekly adds that ink pooling in the cap dries, flakes, and can drop back into the bottle (Pen Weekly).

What about the fridge? Pen Weekly says “generally, no.” A fridge is cold but humid, and moving a bottle into a warm room causes condensation. It also warns never to freeze ink, which “can force pigment out of suspension and crack glass” (Pen Weekly).

Mold and “slime in the bottle”

Mold is the one problem that can spread beyond a single bottle. Goldspot calls it a “worst-case scenario” that “can quickly spread throughout your pen collection and is difficult if not impossible to treat.” It describes two forms: visible mold in the bottle or on the surface, and “slime in the bottle” (SITB), which can hide in the ink until you pull out a slimy nib (Goldspot).

Warning signs, as described by Pen Weekly and Goldspot:

  • stringy threads, floating “islands,” or a rubbery skin on the surface (Pen Weekly);
  • cloudiness or thick slime around the neck (Pen Weekly);
  • a sour or musty smell (Pen Weekly; Goldspot);
  • slime on a toothpick stirred through the ink (Goldspot’s quick check for older bottles) (Goldspot).

If you see these signs, don’t use the ink. Pen Weekly is clear that filtering it isn’t a fix, and putting it in a pen “risks clogging a feed and spreading the problem” (Pen Weekly). If a pen has already touched contaminated ink, flush it thoroughly (see our cleaning guide) and ask the manufacturer or a repairer if you’re worried.

What about sediment?

Sources differ here, so be careful. Pen Weekly says sediment “is not automatically spoilage.” Shimmer inks settle, pigment inks can drop a little solids, and iron gall inks may throw a small amount of sediment over time. A gentle inversion is usually enough (Pen Weekly). Goldspot takes a stricter line: “any ink with sediment in the bottle should not be used in a fountain pen” (Goldspot).

A reasonable middle ground: expected settling in inks designed to settle (shimmer, pigment) is normal if it redistributes with a gentle turn. Unexpected sludge in an ordinary dye ink is a warning sign. Repair specialist Richard Binder notes that dye falling out of solution in the bottle can do the same in your pen and cause a clog (Richard Binder).

Keeping bottles clean while you use them

Contamination usually gets into a bottle on whatever you put into it. Pen Weekly lists dust, nibs that have been sitting around, and residue in syringes as common sources (Pen Weekly). Its suggestions (Pen Weekly):

  • dip only clean, dry nibs into a bottle, or better, decant ink with a clean syringe or pipette instead of dipping;
  • don’t mix inks in the original bottle; use a separate vial;
  • never return used ink to a bottle, whether from a pen, a converter, or a sample vial.

Sample vials

Samples are a cheap way to try inks, but vials are more fragile than bottles. Pen Weekly notes the small volume, the thin plastic, and caps “not always as tight as they look.” Its tips (Pen Weekly):

  • fill vials to just below the neck, leaving little air but not so full that warm days push ink into the threads;
  • label each vial with the ink name, brand, and date;
  • wrap the cap threads with tape or lab film for anything you plan to move or post;
  • store vials upright in a divided tray so one leak doesn’t ruin the rest.

A twice-yearly check

Pen Weekly suggests opening each bottle every six months to look at it, smell it, and check the cap seal, and passing on bottles you haven’t touched in years to someone who will use them (Pen Weekly). It takes ten minutes and catches problems before they reach a pen.

Storing inked pens

Pens are a separate question from bottles. A few guidelines from the sources above:

  • Pens you use often can stay inked. Cap them when not writing so the feed doesn’t dry out (Pen Chalet).
  • Pens you won’t use for a while are best flushed and stored dry. Goulet notes that ink left in an unused pen can dry and cause skipping later (Goulet Pens).
  • Pens with clog-prone, pigmented, or iron gall inks need shorter intervals. Binder suggests not leaving clog-prone inks in a pen for more than a week or so (Richard Binder). Goulet’s listing for a pigmented ink recommends cleaning it out at least weekly (Goulet Pens — Platinum Carbon Black). See our ink safety guide.

Quick checklist

  1. Keep bottles in a dark, cool, stable place, in their boxes and upright.
  2. Don’t refrigerate or freeze ink.
  3. Decant with clean tools. Never pour used ink back.
  4. Label samples and store them upright in a tray.
  5. Check bottles every six months: look, smell, cap.
  6. Discard anything moldy, slimy, or musty-smelling.
  7. Flush pens before long storage.
