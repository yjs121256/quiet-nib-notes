Fountain Pen Nib Sizes Explained: EF, F, M, B, and Why a Japanese Fine Writes Finer

What EF, F, M, and B mean on a fountain pen nib, why Japanese nibs usually run a size finer than Western ones, and how handwriting, paper, and ink change the line you get.

Nib size is the one choice you make when buying a fountain pen that changes how every line looks. It’s also confusing: two pens both labeled “fine” can write noticeably different lines. Google’s suggestions for “fountain pen nib” include “sizes,” “sizes chart,” “types,” and “comparison” (Google autocomplete, checked 2026-11-22), so this is a common question.

This guide explains the letters, the regional difference, and what actually changes the line you see. It doesn’t recommend specific pens.

The basic letters

Most nibs are labeled with one or two letters:

  • EF: extra fine
  • F: fine
  • M: medium
  • B: broad

Some makers offer finer or broader options beyond these, and some use their own labels. These letters describe the tip size, the rounded bit of hard metal at the end of the tines.

JetPens explains that this “tipping” is what touches the paper, and that both steel and gold nibs are “almost always tipped with an alloy of other, harder metals like osmium, tungsten, and ruthenium” (JetPens). The tip’s size and shape set the line width far more than the nib’s metal does.

Which size for everyday writing?

JetPens’ own recommendation for everyday writing is a fine nib. Fine nibs put down less ink, so lines dry more quickly and work better on ordinary copy paper. Their line widths are also similar to typical ballpoint or gel pens, so beginners don’t have to change their handwriting size (JetPens).

The Pen Company adds a rule of thumb about handwriting size: “broad nibs suit larger handwriting styles and fine nibs suit smaller script,” though you may need to experiment (The Pen Company).

Questions to ask yourself:

  • Do you write small, or in narrow-ruled notebooks? Lean finer.
  • Do you write large, sign documents, or want ink color and shading to show? Lean broader.
  • Do you use cheap or thin paper? Finer nibs usually put down less ink and are less likely to bleed through (see the paper section below).
  • Are you left-handed and smudge easily? A finer nib often helps. See our left-handed guide.

Japanese vs. Western sizing

This is the source of most “my fine is too fine” surprises. JetPens explains: “Generally speaking, Western nibs are one nib size broader than Japanese nibs. For example, a Western fine nib is approximately the same size as a Japanese medium nib.” The reason, they note, is that Japanese writing is denser and more complex and needs a finer tip (JetPens).

JetPens publishes typical line widths to illustrate this (JetPens):

EF F M B
Western (typical) 0.5 mm 0.6 mm 0.75 mm 0.9 mm
Japanese (typical) 0.3 mm 0.45 mm 0.6 mm less common

Values as published by JetPens; actual line width varies with ink, paper, and pressure.

There are exceptions. JetPens notes that some Asia-based brands source nibs from European makers and use Western sizing. A few Japanese brands also use Western sizing for specific nibs (JetPens). The label alone doesn’t tell you. Look at writing samples for the exact pen.

Practical translation: if you like a Western fine, a Japanese medium may feel familiar, and vice versa. Treat this as a starting point, not a guarantee.

Tip shape: round, stub, and italic

Size is one variable. Shape is another:

  • Round tips give consistent line width in every direction. JetPens describes round tips as the shape that makes writing “feel smooth” with consistent lines (JetPens). The Pen Company notes that round nibs “create consistent lines regardless of direction whereas italic nibs do not” (The Pen Company).
  • Stub nibs are flatter and wider, with thicker downstrokes than sidestrokes. According to JetPens, they keep some tipping, which softens the edges (JetPens).
  • Italic nibs are flat with no tipping, giving crisper line variation, and they are less forgiving of angle (JetPens).

Beginners usually find round nibs easiest because the pen doesn’t have to be held at a precise orientation.

Steel vs. gold: does it change size or smoothness?

Not the size. On smoothness, JetPens says the nib’s material “doesn’t affect how smooth it is — the nib’s tipping material does.” Gold is softer and springier than steel, which “can help cushion feedback,” but a gold nib is not automatically better (JetPens). A well-tuned steel fine and a well-tuned gold fine can write very similar lines.

JetPens also notes that nib color doesn’t reveal the metal. Gold nibs can be plated silver-colored, and steel nibs can be colored gold (JetPens).

What else changes the line you see

Even with the same nib, line width varies with:

  • Ink: some inks flow more freely (“wetter”) and spread more on paper.
  • Paper: absorbent paper spreads ink and widens lines, while coated, smooth paper keeps them crisper. JetPens recommends fountain-pen-friendly paper for the best results with any pen and ink (JetPens).
  • Pressure: pressing harder spreads the tines on many nibs, widening the line and risking damage. Fountain pens need only light pressure.
  • Angle: changing the angle changes how much of the tip touches the paper. See our holding guide.

This is why reviews and writing samples rarely match exactly. They were made with someone else’s ink, paper, and hand.

Common nib-size mistakes

Buying broad for “smoothness.” Broader tips often feel smoother because more tipping touches the paper. They also put down more ink, which takes longer to dry and is more likely to bleed through thin paper. If smoothness is the goal, a well-finished fine or medium may suit everyday notes better. JetPens points out that tipping quality matters more for smoothness than nib metal (JetPens).

Assuming every “fine” is the same. Because of the regional difference above, a fine from one maker can match a medium or an extra fine from another (JetPens). Compare actual writing samples for the specific model.

Judging a nib on the wrong paper. A nib that feathers on copy paper may be perfectly fine on fountain-pen-friendly paper, and vice versa (JetPens). Test on the paper you’ll use most.

Pressing harder to get a bolder line. If you want a broader line, choose a broader nib rather than forcing a fine one. Extra pressure can spread the tines and, over time, damage the nib.

Choosing without regret

  1. Pick fine as a default if you’re unsure (JetPens).
  2. Check whether the brand uses Japanese or Western sizing, and adjust by about one size if you’re switching between them (JetPens).
  3. Look for pens with swappable nibs if you want to experiment without buying a whole new pen. Some makers sell replacement nibs separately. Check compatibility on the maker’s site.
  4. Test on the paper you actually use. A medium that bleeds on copy paper may look lovely in a good notebook.
