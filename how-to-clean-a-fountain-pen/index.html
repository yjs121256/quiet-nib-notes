How to Clean a Fountain Pen: A Calm, Step-by-Step Flush for Cartridge and Converter Pens

A plain-water flush routine for cartridge and converter fountain pens: when to clean, what you need, the exact steps, drying, and when to stop and get help.

If you have only ever used ballpoints, the idea of “cleaning” a pen can sound like a chore that might break something. It is usually neither difficult nor risky. For most modern cartridge and converter pens, cleaning means one simple thing: pushing clean water through the same path the ink travels until the water comes out clear. The Goulet Pen Company describes it the same way, as repeating the filling process “with clean water instead” of ink (Goulet Pens).

This guide covers the routine flush for everyday pens. It deliberately leaves out disassembling nibs, heat, ultrasonic machines, and vintage pens, because those carry real risk of damage and are better handled by the manufacturer or a repair specialist.

When should you clean a fountain pen?

There is no single schedule that fits every pen and ink, but a few situations come up again and again in manufacturer and retailer guidance.

When the pen is new. Goulet recommends flushing a new pen a couple of times with clean water before inking it, because manufacturing or packaging residue can interfere with flow (Goulet Pens). Pen Chalet gives the same advice: “it never hurts to flush the pen before its first use” (Pen Chalet).

When you change ink. Leftover ink changes the color of the next fill, and some inks do not mix well. A clean pen avoids both problems (Goulet Pens).

When the pen has been sitting. Fountain pen ink is mostly water and evaporates. A pen left inked and unused can start writing dry or skipping (Goulet Pens).

When performance changes. Hard starts, skipping, or needing to press harder are classic signs that the feed needs flushing. Goulet compares cleaning to rebooting a misbehaving device (Goulet Pens).

On a loose routine. Goulet’s rule of thumb is roughly once a month for a pen in regular use, sooner if you change colors or notice a problem (Goulet Pens). Treat this as a starting point, not a law.

Some inks need a shorter interval. Pigmented inks such as Platinum Carbon Black “should not be left to dry out in a pen,” and Goulet’s product listing suggests cleaning them out at least once a week (Goulet Pens — Platinum Carbon Black). Iron gall inks have their own rules, covered in our ink safety guide.

What you need

You do not need special equipment for a routine flush:

  • Clean water. Goulet notes that pen makers generally recommend distilled water because it has the fewest minerals, but bottled or filtered water is often fine, and “any quality of water is still better than not cleaning your pens at all” (Goulet Pens).
  • A cup or two you don’t mind staining, ideally near a sink (Goulet Pens).
  • Absorbent towels: paper towels or a microfiber cloth (Goulet Pens).
  • Optional: a bulb syringe or a commercial pen flush. These help with stubborn ink but are not required for routine cleaning.

Avoid hot water. Fountain Pen Revolution warns that hot water can damage plastic feeds and piston seals (Fountain Pen Revolution). Also avoid needles, pins, and metal picks; they can scratch seals or crack thin plastic (Fountain Pen Revolution).

Step-by-step: flushing a cartridge pen

  1. Protect your work surface. Lay down towels first. Ink stains.
  2. Remove the cartridge. Unscrew the barrel by hand and pull the cartridge straight out. If the barrel doesn’t come off with gentle hand pressure, stop and check the maker’s instructions. If it still has ink, cap or tape the opening, or discard it if it’s nearly empty.
  3. Rinse the section. Hold the front part of the pen (the section, nib, and feed) under a gentle stream of cool water, or dip it in a cup and swish.
  4. Push water through the feed. Dipping alone is not enough. Goulet compares a quick rinse to “going for a walk in the rain instead of taking a shower” (Goulet Pens). If you have a bulb syringe, fit it over the back of the section and gently squeeze water through until it runs out of the nib. If you don’t, an empty cartridge or a converter can do the same job (see below).
  5. Repeat until the water runs clear. Deeply saturated colors may take several rounds.
  6. Blot and dry. Touch the nib to a towel to wick water out of the feed, then let the section dry.

Step-by-step: flushing a converter pen

A converter is a small refillable reservoir that fits where a cartridge would. It makes flushing easy because it works like a pump.

  1. Expel the ink. With the converter still attached, point the nib into a sink or waste cup and turn or push the converter to empty it.
  2. Draw in clean water. Submerge the nib fully in a cup of cool water and draw water up into the converter.
  3. Expel it again. Push the water back out.
  4. Repeat until what comes out is clear. Changing the water in the cup now and then speeds things up.
  5. Remove and rinse the converter itself. Flush it on its own a few times. Fountain Pen Revolution notes that cloudy walls or specks inside a converter can weaken later fills, and suggests a drop of mild dish soap only if water alone doesn’t clear it, followed by thorough rinsing (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  6. Blot the nib and let everything dry.

Don’t skip the drying step

Drying feels like an afterthought, but it is a frequent cause of “my pen writes worse after I cleaned it.” Fountain Pen Revolution lists leftover water diluting fresh ink, damp feeds that don’t saturate evenly, and poorly seated cartridges or converters as common reasons a pen skips right after cleaning. It suggests letting parts dry fully, at least 24 hours in its own guide, before refilling (Fountain Pen Revolution — skipping after cleaning).

You don’t always need to wait that long. If you are refilling with the same ink right away, a few residual drops of water mostly just lighten the first lines. If the pen skips after cleaning, though, drying is the first thing to check.

Stubborn ink: when water isn’t enough

If the water never fully clears, or the pen still writes dry after a good flush, you have a few gentle options:

  • Soak the section (not the whole pen) in clean, room-temperature water for a while, if your pen maker’s instructions allow it, then flush again.
  • Use a commercial pen flush made for fountain pens, following its instructions and flushing it out with water afterward. Goulet’s own tutorial follows that pattern: water first, then the flush solution, then plenty of clean water (Goulet Pens — Pen Flush tutorial).
  • Know which pens not to soak. Repair specialist Richard Binder warns: “Never soak casein!” Casein barrels and caps can be destroyed by soaking. If you own a vintage pen and don’t know its material, don’t soak it (Richard Binder).

Stronger cleaning solutions are described elsewhere for stubborn clogs, but they’re beyond the scope of this beginner guide. Use plain water or a commercial pen flush made for fountain pens, and follow your pen maker’s care instructions. If those don’t clear a clog, ask the manufacturer or a repairer.

When to stop and get help

Cleaning should never require force. Stop and contact the manufacturer, the retailer, or a qualified pen repairer if:

  • the nib or feed is visibly bent, cracked, or misaligned;
  • the section will not unscrew with normal hand pressure;
  • the pen is vintage or uses a sac, lever, or other filling system you don’t recognize;
  • the pen still skips after you have flushed it and let it dry fully.

Pen Chalet notes that a misaligned nib “is best left to an experienced pen tech to repair” (Pen Chalet). That is good advice. A misaligned nib is easy to make worse.

A simple routine you can actually keep

Most people stop cleaning because it feels like a project. Make it small instead:

  • Flush when you change ink.
  • Flush when a pen hasn’t been used for a while.
  • Flush pigmented inks more often, following the ink maker’s guidance.
  • Keep one stained cup and one towel in a drawer specifically for this.

That’s it. A two-minute flush prevents most of the problems covered in our skipping guide and converter troubleshooting guide.
