Fountain Pen Skipping or Hard Starting? A Step-by-Step Diagnosis That Starts With the Safe Fixes

Work through fountain pen skipping in order: ink supply, cap and drying, cleaning, angle and pressure, paper, and only then the nib. Includes when to stop and get help.

A skipping fountain pen is frustrating, and the internet’s advice ranges from “flush it” to “sand the nib.” Those are very different levels of risk. This guide puts the checks in order from safest to most invasive. Most pens are fixed in the first few steps, and the last steps tell you when to hand the pen to someone qualified instead of experimenting.

First, a quick vocabulary check:

  • Skipping: the line breaks up partway through a stroke or word.
  • Hard starting: the pen doesn’t write on the first stroke after a pause, then recovers.
  • Railroading: two thin parallel lines with a gap in the middle, often when writing fast or with pressure.

Pen Chalet describes skipping as what happens when “the feed cannot supply enough ink to the tip of the nib or if the tines on the nib are misaligned.” The first cause is much easier to fix than the second (Pen Chalet). Levenger’s troubleshooting guide groups causes the same way: feed, ink, or nib, each with a different first fix (Levenger).

Step 1: Check the ink supply (one minute)

Before anything else, make sure ink can physically reach the nib.

  • Is there ink? Cartridges and converters can look full when there’s only a film of ink left on the walls.
  • Is the cartridge or converter fully seated? Levenger notes that a loose fit “introduces air gaps that starve the feed” (Levenger). Pull it out and press it back in straight, with firm, even pressure.
  • Was the cartridge pierced? A new cartridge has to be pushed in far enough to open its seal.

If you use a converter, Levenger suggests twisting it slightly to push a small amount of ink into the feed (“priming”) and seeing whether the pen starts writing (Levenger). Do this over a towel.

Step 2: Think about the cap and recent drying

Fountain pen ink dries in contact with air. Pen Chalet reminds readers to cap the pen when not in use, because ink “can dry within the feed and create clogging, skipping and other writing issues” (Pen Chalet). Levenger lists an unsealed cap as a common cause of hard starts (Levenger).

A quick test: dip just the nib (not the whole section) in clean water, blot it, and write. If it improves for a moment, dried ink at the tip is likely, and a proper flush is the next step (Levenger).

Step 3: Flush the pen properly

Cleaning fixes more skipping problems than anything else. Pen Chalet says that “most often a good cleaning of the fountain pen will remedy the problem,” because debris or dried ink in the feed channel blocks flow. New pens can also have leftover residue from manufacturing (Pen Chalet). Goulet Pens likewise lists skipping, hard starts, and needing to press harder as signs a pen needs cleaning (Goulet Pens).

Follow our plain-water cleaning routine, then refill and test.

“It started skipping right after I cleaned it”

This is common and usually temporary. Fountain Pen Revolution lists four typical reasons (Fountain Pen Revolution):

  • leftover water or cleaning solution diluting the fresh ink;
  • a nib and feed that weren’t dry before refilling;
  • a cartridge or converter that wasn’t fully reseated;
  • ink residue that the flush didn’t fully remove.

The fixes are simple: flush again until clear, let the parts dry fully before refilling (FPR suggests at least 24 hours), and reseat the ink reservoir firmly (Fountain Pen Revolution).

Step 4: Check your angle, rotation, and pressure

If the pen is clean and full but still skips, look at how you hold it. A fountain pen only writes well when the tip meets the paper at a workable angle, with the nib’s top facing up.

  • Angle. The Pen Company suggests holding the pen at roughly 40°–55° from the paper and experimenting within that range (The Pen Company). Goulet’s guidance for left-handed writers describes about 45° as ideal and notes that people coming from ballpoints often hold pens too steeply (Goulet Pens — lefties).
  • Rotation. If the pen is rolled so the nib is tilted onto one side, only one tine may touch the paper. Rotate slowly until you find the smooth “sweet spot.”
  • Pressure. Fountain pens need very little pressure. Pressing harder to force ink out can spread the tines and cause railroading, and repeated heavy pressure can bend a nib.

“It only skips on upstrokes”

Pen Chalet points out that upward strokes are “not typically as smooth as downward,” so some difference is normal (Pen Chalet). If upstrokes break up badly, write a few slow, light upward strokes with the nib centered and the top facing up. If they come out clean, your grip or rotation is probably lifting one tine during fast writing. Our guide to holding a fountain pen covers this.

Step 5: Try different paper and a different ink

Paper matters more than people expect. Levenger notes that over-coated or very absorbent paper can cause skipping or drag (Levenger). Test on a smooth, fountain-pen-friendly sheet before deciding the pen is faulty.

Ink matters too. Some inks are “drier” (they flow less freely) than others. Pen Chalet suggests trying a thinner-flowing ink if a clean pen still skips (Pen Chalet), and Levenger suggests pairing fine nibs with moderately wet inks (Levenger). Stick to ink made for fountain pens. See our ink safety guide.

Step 6: Look at the nib, but don’t bend it yet

If you’ve done all of the above and the pen still skips, the nib itself may be the problem. Under good light (a magnifier helps), look at the tip from the front and the side:

  • Are the two tines level? If one tine sits higher than the other, the pen may feel scratchy in one direction. Pen Chalet suggests drawing diagonal lines in four directions to feel for drag (Pen Chalet).
  • Are the tines pinched together at the tip, or spread apart? Either can starve or flood the line.
  • Is the nib sitting snugly on the feed? A visible gap between nib and feed at the front can interrupt flow (Pen Chalet).

Hard starts on a brand-new pen: “baby’s bottom”

One specific cause of hard starts on new pens has an odd name: baby’s bottom. Makoba explains that this happens when the tipping on the nib has been polished so much that it becomes rounded underneath. Paper then touches a part of the nib that sits slightly above the ink path, so the pen writes when pressed or rotated but skips in normal writing (Makoba). Pen Chalet describes it as rounded tines that separate and mentions fixing it with very fine abrasive film (Pen Chalet).

That fix removes metal from the nib, and it can’t be undone. We don’t recommend trying it yourself on a pen you care about.

When to stop and get help

Pen Chalet warns that nib adjustments “may void the warranty” and are not recommended for everyone, especially on expensive pens (Pen Chalet). Stop experimenting and contact the retailer, the manufacturer, or a qualified nib technician if:

  • the pen is new and skips even after a careful flush and a fresh, known-good ink;
  • you see bent, crossed, or uneven tines;
  • the nib has separated from the feed;
  • the pen has been dropped nib-first.

A new pen with a nib problem is often a warranty or exchange question rather than a DIY project.

A one-page checklist

  1. Ink present? Reservoir seated firmly? Cartridge pierced?
  2. Cap left off? Dip the nib in water and test.
  3. Flush until clear. Let it dry. Reseat and refill.
  4. Check angle (roughly 40°–55°), rotation, and light pressure.
  5. Try smooth paper and a different fountain pen ink.
  6. Inspect the tines. If something looks wrong, stop and get help.
