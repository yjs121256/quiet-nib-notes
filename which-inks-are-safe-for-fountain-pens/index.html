Which Inks Are Safe for Fountain Pens? Dye, Pigment, Iron Gall, and the Inks to Avoid

Fountain pens need fountain pen ink. Here's what that means, why India and acrylic inks can ruin a pen, and the extra care pigmented and iron gall inks need.

“Ink is ink” is one of the most expensive assumptions a new fountain pen owner can make. Fountain pens rely on thin channels in the feed and a narrow slit in the nib. The wrong ink can block those channels in a way a simple flush won’t fix. This guide sorts inks into three groups: what’s generally safe, what needs extra care, and what to keep out of a fountain pen entirely.

When in doubt, the pen maker’s instructions and the ink maker’s label take priority over any general guide, including this one.

The rule that covers most cases

Use ink that is sold specifically as fountain pen ink.

Goulet Pens puts this bluntly: fountain pens are designed for water-based fountain pen ink, “NOT calligraphy ink, India ink, acrylic or shellac-based ink of any kind like what is often found at arts & craft stores.” Those inks are made for dip pens and are “too thick to flow through a fountain pen,” so they can clog it and “likely ruin the pen entirely” (Goulet Pens).

Repair specialist Richard Binder explains why traditional fountain pen ink is safe. It is a solution, with dyes fully dissolved in water along with wetting agents and mold inhibitors, so it “contains no solid matter at all” (Richard Binder). Particles that don’t dissolve can lodge in the feed.

Group 1: Standard dye-based fountain pen inks (generally safe)

Most bottled and cartridge fountain pen inks are dye-based. Binder states that “any ink designed for fountain pens is safe for use in your pens,” with some caveats worth knowing (Richard Binder):

  • Highly saturated inks can drop dye out of solution. Binder notes that some very saturated “boutique” inks can leave sludge at the bottom of the bottle, and that the same can happen inside a pen and cause a clog. If you have to shake or stir a bottle because of sludge, he recommends caution. He also suggests not leaving pens filled with clog-prone inks for more than a week or so (Richard Binder).
  • Some colors stain more than others. Binder observes that saturated inks stain more, that violets and reds tend to stain most, and that blues and blacks stain least. This matters if your pen has a clear barrel or ink window (Richard Binder).
  • Don’t concentrate ink by evaporating it. Binder recommends against this, because it creates the same over-saturation problem (Richard Binder).

None of these is a reason to fear dye inks. They’re reasons to flush your pen when you change colors or put it away.

Group 2: Inks that are made for fountain pens but need extra care

Pigmented fountain pen inks

Some inks are pigmented: tiny solid particles suspended in liquid, rather than fully dissolved dye. They’re often chosen for permanence and water resistance. Goulet’s listing for Platinum Carbon Black describes it as pigmented rather than dye-based, which makes it “very permanent and lightfast,” but notes it “does require more diligent maintenance” (Goulet Pens — Platinum Carbon Black).

The same listing is specific about the care: pigmented inks “should not be left to dry out in a pen,” and it’s “best to clean it from your pen at least once a week to prevent clogging.” It adds that “certain pens may handle pigmented inks better than others” (Goulet Pens — Platinum Carbon Black).

In practice: use pigmented fountain pen inks in pens you write with often, flush on the ink maker’s schedule, and don’t let them dry in the pen.

Iron gall inks

Iron gall inks darken as they dry on paper and are valued for permanence. Sources describe the risk in slightly different ways, so it’s worth reading more than one.

Goulet’s product glossary describes iron gall inks as “quite permanent and waterproof” but “typically known for causing chemical corrosion to metal pen parts (especially the nib),” and suggests using them “sparingly or with less-valuable pens” (Goulet Pens — product attributes).

Nibhaven’s iron gall guide takes a more permissive view. It says iron gall ink is safe in most modern fountain pens with regular cleaning, and that the real hazard is ink drying and concentrating inside the pen. It reports that Rohrer & Klingner recommends weekly cleaning for pens inked with its iron gall products, and suggests flushing before storing any pen that won’t be used for more than a week (Nibhaven). It also recommends avoiding iron gall in pens that dry out quickly, and notes that ink maker KWZ advises against mixing iron gall with red, brown, or purple inks (Nibhaven).

In practice: both sources agree on the core rule: don’t let iron gall ink dry inside a pen. Choose an iron gall ink made specifically for fountain pens, follow its maker’s cleaning advice, and, if you’re cautious, start with a pen you wouldn’t mind replacing.

Shimmer inks

Some fountain pen inks contain shimmer particles. Fountain Pen Revolution lists “tiny bits of shimmer” among residues that can build up in a feed and cause skipping (Fountain Pen Revolution). Pen Weekly notes that shimmer inks settle in the bottle and need a gentle inversion before use (Pen Weekly). Treat them like pigmented inks: regular use, regular flushing.

Group 3: Inks to keep out of fountain pens

Based on the Goulet guidance above, avoid putting these in a fountain pen unless the pen maker specifically says otherwise (Goulet Pens):

  • India ink (traditional drawing ink)
  • Calligraphy and dip-pen inks
  • Acrylic inks
  • Shellac-based inks
  • Craft-store “drawing” inks whose label doesn’t say “fountain pen”

Some products blur the lines, such as drawing inks marketed as “fountain pen safe.” Rely on the ink maker’s explicit statement, not on a color name or a similar-looking bottle.

Mixing inks

Mixing different inks in a pen, even fountain pen inks, can cause unexpected reactions. Goulet warns that some inks “don’t mix well with each other,” which is one reason to flush between colors (Goulet Pens). Pen Weekly suggests mixing in a separate vial rather than in the original bottle (Pen Weekly). Nibhaven reports KWZ’s warning against mixing iron gall with certain colors (Nibhaven).

The safe default is one ink at a time, with a flush in between.

A quick decision guide

Ink type Safe in fountain pens? What to do
Dye-based fountain pen ink Yes Flush when changing colors; be aware of staining colors
Very saturated “boutique” dye ink Generally, with care Don’t leave it sitting in a pen for long periods
Pigmented fountain pen ink Yes, if labeled for fountain pens Use often; clean on the maker’s schedule (Goulet: at least weekly for Platinum Carbon Black)
Iron gall fountain pen ink Yes, if labeled for fountain pens Never let it dry in the pen; follow the maker’s cleaning schedule
Shimmer fountain pen ink Yes, if labeled for fountain pens Invert gently; flush regularly
India, calligraphy, acrylic, shellac inks No Keep for dip pens and brushes

If you’ve already put the wrong ink in

Don’t let it dry. Remove the cartridge or converter, flush the section with plenty of clean water right away, and keep flushing. If it doesn’t clear, or the pen won’t write afterward, contact the manufacturer or a pen repairer before trying anything aggressive. See our cleaning guide for the basic flush.
