Fountain Pen Converter Not Filling? Diagnose It by What You See

Converter drawing air, only a few drops, or nothing? Start with a water test, then follow the branch that matches what you see: seal and fit, submersion, or feed and residue.

A converter is a small refillable reservoir that fits into a cartridge pen so you can use bottled ink. “Fountain pen converter not filling” shows up as a Google search suggestion (Google autocomplete, checked 2026-11-22). That’s no surprise. When it fails, the first fill of a new pen can go from a few seconds to a puzzling half-hour.

Most troubleshooting lists go through causes one by one. This guide does it the other way round: it starts with what you actually see and sends you down one short branch. The goal is to find the problem without disassembling anything, using hot water or sharp tools, or forcing parts.

Ground rules before you start

  • Use room-temperature water only. Fountain Pen Revolution (FPR), which sells pens and converters, warns that hot water can damage plastic feeds and piston seals, and that needles or metal picks can crack thin plastic or scratch seals (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  • Don’t take apart the nib, feed, or converter unless the maker’s instructions say that’s intended.
  • Work over a towel. Even water tests drip.

Step 0: The water test

Before blaming the converter, take ink out of the picture. Fill a small cup with clean, room-temperature water. Remove any old cartridge, fit the converter, push the plunger all the way down, submerge the nib and the front of the section, and turn or pull the plunger slowly. FPR recommends testing with water and watching for large bubbles near the joint between converter and pen (Fountain Pen Revolution).

Then look closely. What happened falls into one of four patterns:

What you see in the water test Go to
Steady bubbles, or the converter fills mostly with air Branch A: air is getting in
A little water enters, then it stops Branch B: the nib isn’t staying submerged, or you’re turning too fast
The plunger moves but almost nothing enters Branch C: something is blocking the path
The plunger itself feels stiff, slips, or jerks Branch D: the converter mechanism
Water fills fine, but ink doesn’t Branch E: it’s the ink or the bottle

If several apply, start with A. It’s the most common and the easiest to fix.

Branch A: Air is getting in

Air enters wherever the seal is weakest. With a converter, that’s usually the joint where it meets the pen.

  1. Check the converter matches the pen. Converters are not universal, and FPR’s first check is compatibility with the pen’s connector (Fountain Pen Revolution). The reliable reference is the pen maker’s own listing or manual. A converter that “fits” by pushing in isn’t necessarily the right one.
  2. Reseat it straight. Pull the converter out and press it back in along the pen’s axis, without twisting it at an angle. A wobble or tilt after seating suggests it isn’t fully home or isn’t the right part (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  3. Dry the joint. A film of water or ink on the converter’s nipple can stop it sealing. Dry both parts with a tissue, reseat, and repeat the water test. FPR suggests the same: dry, retest, then reseat or replace (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  4. Look for damage. A crack in the converter or a deformed nipple won’t seal however carefully you seat it. That’s a replacement part, not a repair.

Result: if bubbles stop after reseating, you’re done. If they don’t, compare against the maker’s specified converter before going further.

Branch B: It starts filling, then stops

Here the seal is usually fine, but the pen is drawing air at the end of the stroke because the ink inlet isn’t fully submerged.

  • Go deeper. The whole nib and the front of the feed need to stay under the surface for the whole stroke. FPR’s advice is to keep the nib and feed fully submerged and turn slowly (Fountain Pen Revolution). In practice, that’s often deeper than people expect.
  • Slow down. A fast stroke pulls air in through any gap. Turn or pull the plunger slowly enough that you can watch the level rise.
  • Purge and repeat. Push a few drops back out while the nib is still submerged in the same cup of water, then draw again. This pushes out trapped air. (With ink, see the contamination note in Branch E.)

Result: if water now fills properly, your technique was the issue, not the hardware. Repeat the same motion with ink.

Branch C: The plunger moves, but almost nothing enters

If the mechanism moves freely and the joint seals, but very little comes in, something may be restricting flow through the feed. That’s common in pens that have dried out or been inked for a long time.

  1. Flush the section on its own. Remove the converter and flush the nib and feed with room-temperature water. FPR recommends water or a pen flush here (Fountain Pen Revolution). Our cleaning guide covers the methods.
  2. Allow time for dried ink. Ink that has dried in a feed may take more than one flush to clear. Repeat until the water runs clear rather than forcing water through harder.
  3. Retest with water. If the fill improves, the feed was the bottleneck.

If repeated plain-water flushes don’t help, stop. A pen that still won’t draw after thorough flushing is a question for the manufacturer or a repairer, especially if it’s vintage or valuable. Some older pens need special care. Richard Binder, a pen repairer, warns that soaking can destroy casein barrels and caps (Richard Binder).

Branch D: The converter mechanism itself

Converters are mechanical. If the plunger is stiff, slips, jerks, or doesn’t move the seal inside, the converter itself may be the problem.

  • Flush the converter on its own. Draw clean water in and out several times with the converter off the pen. Dried ink around the seal can make it stiff.
  • Look at the seal while it moves. On a clear converter you can often see whether the seal travels with the knob. If the knob turns but the seal doesn’t move, the converter isn’t drawing anything.
  • Know when to replace it. FPR mentions lubricating dry piston seals with a thin film of silicone grease (Fountain Pen Revolution). Whether that’s appropriate depends on the converter, so follow the maker’s guidance. Many converters are inexpensive, so check the replacement price before spending time on a stiff one.

Branch E: Water fills fine, but ink doesn’t

If the hardware passes the water test, the problem is with the ink or the bottle.

  • Low bottle. A nearly empty bottle may not be deep enough to submerge the nib. FPR suggests tilting the bottle or moving the ink to a small vial (Fountain Pen Revolution). Pen Weekly recommends a clean syringe or pipette for decanting (Pen Weekly).
  • Don’t contaminate the bottle. Pen Weekly warns never to return ink from a pen, converter, or vial to the main bottle (Pen Weekly). If you purge drops to remove air, it’s cleaner to fill from a separate vial than to push used ink back into the bottle. More in our ink storage guide.
  • Check the ink itself. Ink that’s thickened, become slimy, or that isn’t actually fountain pen ink can fill poorly and may harm the pen. See which inks are safe for fountain pens.

Residue: the slow-building version of Branches C and D

Converters that have been used for a long time sometimes look cloudy or speckled inside. FPR lists residue among its common causes and suggests flushing, with a drop of mild dish soap if needed (Fountain Pen Revolution). If you use soap, rinse very thoroughly so none reaches your next fill, and don’t use soap if the pen or converter maker advises against it.

After a successful fill

Once the converter is full, hold the pen nib-up and turn the plunger a fraction to push out any air bubble near the feed, then wipe the nib. Write a few lines on scrap paper. If the pen fills well but skips or writes faintly, the converter probably isn’t the problem. Move on to our skipping diagnosis.

When to stop

Stop and ask the manufacturer or a repairer if:

  • the pen still won’t draw water after several room-temperature flushes;
  • you see cracks, a deformed connector, or a loose section;
  • the pen is vintage, valuable, or under warranty and the fix would mean opening anything.

A converter is usually the cheapest part of the system. A cracked feed or a voided warranty isn’t.
