Setting Up a New Fountain Pen for the First Time: Flush, Fill, and Your First Lines

A beginner's first-hour guide: why to flush a new pen, how to insert a cartridge or fill a converter, how to get ink to the nib, and what's normal in the first few lines.

A new fountain pen usually comes with a cartridge or two, sometimes a converter, and very little instruction. The search suggestions for “how to fill a fountain pen” include “for the first time” and “without a converter,” which says a lot about how confusing that first hour can be (Google autocomplete, checked 2026-11-22). This guide walks through it slowly.

It covers cartridge/converter pens, the most common type for beginners. Piston fillers, eyedroppers, and vintage lever or sac pens work differently. Follow the maker’s instructions for those.

What’s in the box (and what each part does)

  • Nib: the metal tip that touches the paper.
  • Feed: the usually black plastic piece under the nib. It carries ink from the reservoir to the nib by capillary action. JetPens describes ink flowing “down the nib’s slit” thanks to the liquid’s surface tension (JetPens).
  • Section: the part you hold, which the nib and feed fit into.
  • Barrel and cap: the body that screws or pushes onto the section, and the cover that protects the nib.
  • Cartridge: a sealed, disposable ink capsule.
  • Converter (optional): a refillable reservoir that fits where a cartridge goes, so you can use bottled ink.

Step 1: Flush the new pen with water

It feels odd to clean a pen you haven’t used, but it’s widely recommended. Goulet Pens advises flushing a new pen a couple of times with clean water before inking, because residue from manufacturing or packaging can affect flow (Goulet Pens). Pen Chalet agrees: “it never hurts to flush the pen before its first use” (Pen Chalet).

If you have a converter, this is easy: attach it, dip the nib in clean water, draw water in, push it out, and repeat a few times. If you only have cartridges, run cool water gently through the section and let it drip. Our cleaning guide has the full routine.

Then blot the nib on a towel and let the section dry for a while. Leftover water dilutes the first fill and can cause skipping, a common complaint right after cleaning (Fountain Pen Revolution).

Step 2a: Insert a cartridge

  1. Unscrew the barrel from the section.
  2. Check which end of the cartridge goes in. It’s the end with the sealed opening (often a small ball or membrane). The closed end faces the back of the pen.
  3. Push the cartridge straight into the section with steady pressure until you feel the seal give and the cartridge seats. Don’t twist it at an angle. If it won’t go in with moderate hand pressure, check it’s the right cartridge for the pen rather than pushing harder.
  4. Screw the barrel back on.

Levenger’s troubleshooting guide notes that cartridges need to be “fully punctured and seated,” and that a loose fit creates air gaps that starve the feed (Levenger).

Is my cartridge compatible? Cartridges are often brand-specific. Some pens use a widely shared “international” size, while others use proprietary shapes. Check the pen maker’s product page or manual before buying spare cartridges from another brand.

Step 2b: Fill a converter from a bottle

  1. Push the converter firmly into the section, the same way you would a cartridge. Fountain Pen Revolution notes that converters aren’t universal and suggests a gentle tug-and-twist test for a secure fit (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  2. Turn or push the converter’s mechanism to empty it fully.
  3. Dip the pen into the ink until the whole nib and the front of the section are under the surface. FPR’s guidance is to keep “nib and feed fully submerged” (Fountain Pen Revolution).
  4. Slowly turn the converter to draw ink in. Slow is the key word. Fast strokes pull in air.
  5. Lift the pen out, wipe the nib and section with a tissue, and reassemble.

If the converter fills with bubbles or barely fills, work through our converter troubleshooting guide.

Step 3: Get ink to the nib

A cartridge pen is often ready to write after a short wait, because the feed has to fill with ink first. Some ways to help it along:

  • Wait a few minutes with the pen capped, resting on a towel. Some people rest it nib-down to help the feed fill. If you do, keep it capped and check it after a few minutes rather than leaving it.
  • Very gently squeeze the cartridge, only if it’s a soft plastic cartridge you can reach and the maker doesn’t advise against it, to push a little ink forward. Do this over a towel, and stop at the first drop. Never squeeze hard.
  • With a converter, turn the piston a tiny amount to push a drop of ink into the feed. Levenger calls this “priming” (Levenger).

Don’t shake the pen hard to force ink down. That’s a quick way to splatter ink.

Step 4: Your first lines

Hold the pen lightly with the top of the nib facing up, at a moderate angle to the paper. The Pen Company suggests trying angles around 40°–55° until you find the smooth “sweet spot” (The Pen Company). Use much less pressure than you would with a ballpoint. The nib only needs to touch the paper. Our holding guide covers grip in more detail.

Write some loops, straight lines, and a few words. Here’s what’s normal in the first minutes:

  • Pale or watery lines at first: often leftover flushing water mixing with ink. It usually clears up as you write.
  • A slight scratchy feel on upstrokes: some feedback in upward strokes is normal. Pen Chalet notes that upstrokes are “not typically as smooth as downward” (Pen Chalet).
  • Lines that look finer or broader than you expected: nib sizes vary by brand and region. Japanese fine nibs, for example, often write finer than Western fine nibs (JetPens). See our nib size guide.

Some signs need attention: the pen won’t write at all after several minutes, the line breaks up constantly, or ink pools around the section. Start with our skipping diagnosis.

Step 5: Choose the right ink (and avoid the wrong ones)

Use ink made for fountain pens. Goulet warns that fountain pens are designed for water-based fountain pen ink, “NOT calligraphy ink, India ink, acrylic or shellac-based ink,” which are made for dip pens and can clog and ruin a fountain pen (Goulet Pens). If you’re not sure, read our ink safety guide before buying a bottle.

Step 6: Habits that keep the pen happy

  • Cap the pen when you’re not writing. Pen Chalet notes that uncapped pens can dry out in the feed (Pen Chalet).
  • Use it regularly, or flush it before storing. Ink left sitting evaporates and thickens (Goulet Pens).
  • Flush when changing ink colors (Goulet Pens).
  • Don’t lend it to a heavy-handed writer. Heavy pressure can spread or bend the tines.

Quick-start summary

  1. Flush the new pen with clean water. Blot and let it dry.
  2. Insert a cartridge firmly, or fill a converter slowly with the nib fully submerged.
  3. Let the feed saturate: cap it, rest it nib-down, or prime gently.
  4. Write lightly, top of the nib up, at a moderate angle.
  5. Use fountain-pen ink only, and cap the pen between uses.
